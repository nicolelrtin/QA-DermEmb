Box 1.1The Directional Score

Each answer is mapped to a signed confidence and standardised per question across images. Every later chapter uses this representation.

Because the score keeps the stated confidence, a question that is almost never affirmed can still vary informatively across images.

II
Structure of the questions

How many independent things do the 500 questions measure, and which questions go together?

Principal component analysis with parallel analysis estimates how many dimensions of the answers exceed shuffled data. Agglomerative (Ward) clustering on the correlation between answers then shows what those dimensions contain.

Fig. 2.1Variance explained by each component, against parallel analysis

Explained variance per principal component (log scale). Dashed: 95th percentile from 50 column-wise permutations of the answers, the variance a component would explain by chance.

Fig. 2.2Agglomerative clustering of the questions

Pearson correlation between every pair of questions across the 1,000 images, ordered by Ward clustering on correlation distance (1 − r); the dendrogram above shows the merge order. The grey strip alternates shade between clusters at the chosen k. Hover for the two questions; select a row to open its question.

Order

Fig. 2.3The question clusters and their subtype profiles

Clusters from Fig. 2.2, ordered by how well the cluster's mean score separates malignant from benign images (|AUROC − 0.5|). Cells: the cluster's mean z-score in each subtype (red above, blue below the average image). Select a row to list its questions.

Clusters correspond to dermoscopic families (vascular patterns, keratinocytic features, pigment network, blue-grey structures) and to the perilesional skin (photodamage, actinic change). Artefact clusters are the least coherent.

III
The classifier

Do the answers discriminate malignant from benign, and how does the model compare with others?

L2-penalised logistic regression (ridge) on the standardised Directional Scores of the 446 clinical questions, evaluated by nested cross-validation with folds grouped by patient. It is compared on identical folds with the model's direct answer to “Is this a malignant lesion?”, with models trained on other targets, and with SkinGPT-R1.

    IV
    The ten subtypes

    How well are the ten diagnostic subtypes distinguished, and melanoma from naevus among them?

    The ridge model of Ch. III, trained to predict all ten subtypes at once (100 images each) under the same patient-grouped nested cross-validation. Each image is assigned to the subtype of highest predicted probability; chance is 10%. The second part reads from the same model the distinction of greatest clinical consequence among pigmented lesions, naevus against melanoma.

    V
    Interpretation

    Which questions carry the decision, and how is a single prediction formed?

    Importance is ranked by consensus across six model families for malignant against benign (stage S7), with the standardised ridge weight giving the direction; the marker questions of each subtype come from the ten-subtype ridge model. Two worked cases then show how one prediction is assembled from the answers. Selecting any question opens it.

    Fig. 5.1The twenty highest-ranked questions

    Weight: standardised ridge coefficient (red toward malignant, blue toward benign). Mean P(yes) in benign and malignant images. Alone: univariate AUROC. Kept: selection frequency over 25 lasso fits, a measure of how stable the question's contribution is. doubt affirmed in fewer than 5% of images of every class, so the signal lies in the confidence of the negative answer.

    Largest positive coefficients for each class in the ten-subtype ridge model, with mean P(yes) within the subtype against all others.
