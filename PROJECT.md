# QA-DermEmb: project notes

Nicole Tin, AbbasiLab, UCSF. Started 2026-10-08; QA-Emb pipeline since 2026-10-23.

This file records where the project stands: what we are trying to do, the decisions currently in force and why, and what comes next. What we have learned from experiments is in [FINDINGS.md](FINDINGS.md). The detailed evidence is in the reports under [reports/](reports/README.md).

## Aim

Represent each image by a model's answers to about 500 curated yes/no dermatology questions (QA-Emb; Benara et al. 2024, arXiv 2405.16714). Then classify malignant against benign from those answers, and show where in the image the influential questions apply. The point is a classifier whose features a dermatologist can read and check.

Performance target (PI meeting, 2026-11-12): clinicians would find an AUROC above 0.9 for benign against malignant meaningful, so that is the number to beat. The first real run reached 0.84 (ridge on the Directional Score, cross-validated; FINDINGS 18).

Paper framing (PI meeting, 2026-11-12): the introduction will argue that saliency methods such as Grad-CAM are not a sufficient form of interpretability. They show where a model looked, but not what it saw there, and a clinician cannot check that against their own reading of the lesion. Readable question answers are meant to fill that gap.

Narrative of the paper, as planned on 2026-11-12 after the PI and post-doc meetings. Each part names the analysis that supplies it.
1. Saliency maps show where, not what (introduction, above).
2. The answers can be trusted as measurements. Claude answers a question and its negation consistently, where the local models contradict themselves (FINDINGS 2–4; fig1 of the [2026-10-30 report](reports/2026-10-30_claude_poc/README.md), to be redone on 10 beta images; Plan item C), and agrees with itself across replicates and with Opus (FINDINGS 5, 11).
3. The answers have structure. Questions in the same topic are answered alike, partly because they describe related features and partly because they are asked in the same prompt; we measure how much of each (Plan items B and D). This also tells us how many independent features the 500 questions really amount to.
4. The answers classify. Ridge on the Directional Score against standard image models and other interpretable models on the same folds (Plan items A and E, Benchmarks below).
5. The classifier can be checked against medical knowledge. Answer vectors derived from the literature, with no image involved, serve as positive controls for the classifier and as a knowledge-only benchmark; the same questionnaire could be filled in by a dermatologist (Plan item F).
6. External test set, once (Plan item 6).

## Plan

### Next

Immediate steps agreed with the PI on 2026-11-12. These come before items 4 to 6 below.

A. Tune ridge regression and report its performance. Done 2026-11-14 ([report](reports/2026-11-14_ridge_tuning/README.md), FINDINGS 20), with a new stage, S8 tune. The PI asked what the ridge penalty (lambda) is and how we choose it. Lambda is the strength of the L2 penalty on the standardised question weights; scikit-learn parametrises it as C = 1/lambda. For each outer fold, C is chosen by 3-fold cross-validation inside that fold's training images, scored by log loss, with classes weighted equally ([qaemb/models/cv.py](qaemb/models/cv.py)). S6 searched C in {0.0001, 0.0003, 0.001, 0.003, 0.01, 0.03, 0.1} (lambda 10 to 10,000); the benign-against-malignant model on the Directional Score chose lambda about 33 in 22 of 25 folds.
   - Grid refined (21 values, lambda 1 to 100,000) and the inner and outer scores plotted against lambda. One broad optimum at lambda 20 to 100; outer AUROC 0.840 with the fine grid against 0.839 with S6's, and 0.841 for the best value in hindsight. The fine grid is to be adopted at the next S6 rerun (Plan item 4); it does not justify a rerun on its own.
   - Selecting lambda by AUROC instead of log loss: decided against. No better (0.839), and the choice scatters more, because AUROC on an inner fold of about 210 images moves in steps. The one-standard-error rule was worse (0.829).
   - Repeats: the evaluation is already nested cross-validation; the "5 repeats" are repeats of the outer 5-fold split with different shuffles. Each repeat alone is a 5×3 nested CV, and over the 5 repeats AUROC ranged from 0.832 to 0.842. We keep the repeats, since dropping them would make the reported number depend on one shuffle by about 0.01 and saves little time. Still to confirm with the PI that this is what he meant.
   - Final model done 2026-11-15 in S10 ([report](reports/2026-11-15_ridge_final/README.md)): fitted on all labelled images with lambda by grouped CV on them, frozen, not scored.
   - "Test set performance": we have no external test set yet, so the outer-fold scores of the nested CV are the performance we report. The final model, refitted on all 1,000 images with lambda chosen on those same images, is not scored on them. The PI (2026-11-12): "for the final model then, make sure not to report performance because there will be leakage between validation and test sets". Its performance is reported only on the external test set.

D. Covariance of answers within topics. First half done 2026-11-15 in the S9 explore run (FINDINGS 21): same-topic pairs correlate at 0.55 against 0.10 between topics and 0.12 for random groups, and the gap is about 0.4 at every level of wording similarity; 27 dimensions above noise. The stateless comparison below is now the main open step. Because each topic is asked in one prompt, questions in the same topic may be answered alike for two reasons: they describe related features, or Sonnet keeps its answers consistent within one prompt. On the 1,000 images, compare the correlations of the Directional Score between questions in the same topic with those between topics, against random groupings of the same sizes, and cluster the questions hierarchically by their answers (shared with item B). To separate the two reasons, ask the questions one per prompt (stateless) on a subset of about 20 images and compare the within-topic correlations of the two modes; FINDINGS 6 measured agreement between the modes, not correlation. Also estimate how many independent dimensions the answers have (for example the number of principal components needed for 90% of the variance), since this bears on how many questions the classifier really uses.

E. Benchmarks on the same folds as ridge: SkinGPT-R1's own diagnosis as a classifier; SkinGPT-R1's image embedding with ridge on top; the interpretable model once chosen (open question below); later the literature benchmark (item F). Each benchmark is fitted and tuned inside the same outer folds, so the scores are paired by fold.

F. Literature-derived answer vectors as positive controls and as a benchmark (post-doc meeting, 2026-11-12; most important of the new items). Claude Science compiles the literature in phases, and stops for our review after the search and after a pilot ([prompt](inputs/prompts/claude_science_literature_controls_v1.md)). It produces:
   - question annotations: for each question and each malignant diagnosis, whether the feature is the visible correlate of a documented process in the lesion (1, mechanistic), only associated with the diagnosis (0.5), or unrelated (0), and in which direction (towards malignant or towards benign), with a quoted source;
   - case vectors: about 100–150 histopathologically confirmed published cases over the ten classes, each answered three times. The presence reading answers from what the authors say the lesion looks like. The mechanistic reading answers from the histopathology, through the mechanistic links in the annotations, without using the described appearance. The image reading answers from the case's published figure alone, and is made by our own S2 stage with Sonnet at the same settings as the beta images, so it comes from the same instrument as the classifier's training data (about $0.20 per image at the beta-run rate, so roughly $30 for 150 cases). Claude Science extracts the figures only after both text readings are finished, so the images cannot influence them.
   - feature frequencies: how often each feature occurs in each diagnosis where studies report it, and a canonical textbook profile per class.

   Every answer has the same two inputs as Sonnet's: P(yes) and a confidence. The prompt gives fixed anchors so the numbers mean the same thing across cases (stated present: P(yes) 0.95, confidence 0.9; stated absent: 0.05, 0.9; hedged, caption-only and implied answers at lower confidence). A question the source says nothing about is recorded as P(yes) 0.5 with confidence 0. The Directional Score, P(yes) and the Coherence Score then follow by the same formulas as for the images.

   We then use them in four ways, built as a pipeline stage. First, as positive controls: score the case vectors (each reading) and vectors sampled from the frequency table with the frozen ridge models, and check that the malignant ones are called malignant. Second, as a knowledge-only benchmark: a score from the annotation weights alone (no fitting to our images, like the 7-point checklist) applied to Sonnet's answers on the 1,000 images, compared with ridge on the same folds. Third, compare ridge's question weights with the literature annotations, to see whether the classifier relies on the features the literature names (FINDINGS 19 suggests it partly does not). Fourth, compare the readings of the same lesion: where the authors describe a feature (presence reading), does Sonnet report it from the figure (image reading)? This speaks directly to the open question of whether Sonnet's graded "no" reflects visual evidence. Comparing the mechanistic and presence readings shows how far a case's description follows from its pathology. A dermatologist could later fill in the same questionnaire from cases, which would give a human version of the second use.

   A missing answer cannot simply enter ridge as confidence 0. Confidence 0 gives a Directional Score of 0, but Sonnet's scores on the 1,000 images are mostly confident "no"s: the middle half lie between −0.83 and −0.32, and the per-question median is −0.61 (embed `6cd6f95ebcbd`, checked 2026-11-12). For the median question a score of 0 is about 3 standard deviations above Sonnet's average, and for two thirds of the questions it is more than 2. That is exactly the "unusually doubtful no" that the classifier reads as malignancy (FINDINGS 19), so a sparse vector would be pushed towards malignant whatever the case is, and a positive control would pass for the wrong reason. We therefore keep confidence 0 as the stored record, but score in two ways and report both. In the neutral version, each missing answer is replaced by Sonnet's average for that question in the training fold, which after standardisation contributes nothing to the ridge score. In the literal version, it is a score of 0. As a check on the method, an empty vector (every answer missing) is scored too: in the neutral version it lands at the model's baseline, and in the literal version it shows how large the bias is. The scores can also be computed on the answered questions only, for each case.

## Open questions

- Which Fisher-information-based interpretable model to use as a benchmark. Raised 2026-11-12 (PI meeting); needs a literature search.

- Does the literature benchmark reward the classifier for using the "right" features, or only for the right answer? A classifier that relies on surrounding skin and graded doubt (FINDINGS 18, 19) could still call the literature vectors malignant if a few textbook features carry enough weight. The comparison of ridge's weights with the literature annotations (Plan item F) is meant to tell these apart. Raised 2026-11-12.

- When Sonnet answers "no" with more or less doubt (P(yes) 0.02 against 0.10) to a feature it almost never reports, does that reflect visual evidence for the feature or only its overall impression of the lesion? Much of the classifiers' signal is of this kind (FINDINGS 19), and the answer decides how far a question's importance can be read as "this feature was present". Raised 2026-11-11.

- Does Sonnet answer a topic's questions from one overall impression? Questions in the same prompt are answered far more alike than their wording explains (FINDINGS 21). If the impression drives the answers, a question's importance says less about the feature it names. Raised 2026-11-15; the stateless subset (Plan item D) tests it.

- How much do the cross-validated scores change when the folds are grouped by patient and when clinical photographs are scored separately? Raised and answered 2026-11-15 (FINDINGS 23): grouping by patient leaves benign against malignant at 0.84 and lowers the 10-subtype score by 0.01; dermoscopic images alone score 0.82. Whether a model trained on dermoscopic images only does better on them is still open.

- How much of the classifiers' accuracy comes from the patient's skin around the lesion (sun damage, skin tone, roughness) rather than the lesion itself, and will that carry over to the external test set? Raised 2026-11-11 (FINDINGS 18).

- Are the negated phrasings ("Does the lesion lack …") partly to blame for the local models' contradictions? Rewriting a sample as positive opposites ("Is the border regular?") would tell us. Raised 2026-10-27; not yet tested.
- Do Claude's stated probabilities and confidences mean the same thing across images, or only rank answers within an image? This matters more now that confidence replaces the negated questions.
- How far the external test set's sources and its mix of dermoscopic and clinical images differ from the beta set. It will come from another ISIC batch and from internal data (settled 2026-11-12).
